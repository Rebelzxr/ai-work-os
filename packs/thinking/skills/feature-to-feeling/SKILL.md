# Feature to feeling

Flat copy *transmits* features ("cloud sync", "24/7 support", "API integration"). Copy that converts *translates* a feature into a felt moment, then into identity. The reader's real question is never "what does this do" — it's "what does this have to do with me."

## The move

**Feature → felt value → identity.** Lead every hook, headline, subject line and first sentence with the felt moment, never the mechanism. The mechanism can follow, once the reader already feels seen.

## Steps

1. List the literal features in the draft (what it does, in the vendor's own words).
2. For each, ask: what does this let the reader stop worrying about, or stop having to do? That's the felt value.
3. For the strongest one or two, ask: what does having this say about the kind of business/person the reader is (or becomes)? That's the identity layer.
4. Rewrite the opening line to lead with the felt value or identity, not the feature. Move the mechanism later, or into a supporting line.
5. Check: does the new opening read like it's about the reader's life, or still about the product?

## Worked example

Before: "Our platform offers automated invoice reminders and payment tracking."
Feature: automated reminders. Felt value: never chasing a client for money again. Identity: the kind of owner who doesn't have to be the one nagging people.
After: "Stop being the person who has to chase your own clients for money. Invoices remind themselves — you just get paid."

## Failure modes

- Do not invent a benefit the product doesn't actually deliver — the felt value has to be true of the real feature.
- Do not strip out the mechanism entirely; a reader who feels seen still wants to know it's real. Put the "how" after the hook, not instead of it.
- Watch for copy that stays in third person and distant ("businesses can now...") — feeling-first copy usually speaks to "you."
